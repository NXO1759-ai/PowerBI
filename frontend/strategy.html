    Industry structure

    Structure determines how the value an industry creates gets divided — and it sets profitability over the medium and long run, where the business cycle only moves the short run. The figures below are the consistency check on a structural read, not a substitute for one.

    Where returns sit

    Return on invested capital is the measure strategy uses, because margins and profit growth both ignore the capital a business needs to compete. Industries are placed against Porter's published distribution of US industry returns rather than scored — the point is to understand what drives the number, not to grade it.

    The profit pool

    Each firm is a rectangle: width is its share of industry revenue, height its operating margin, so the area is the profit it takes. Revenue and profit rarely concentrate in the same place, and where they diverge is where the strategy question lives.

    Competitive position

    Where this company sits among its peers, and whether it stayed there. A single good year is not an advantage; holding a position across a cycle is.

    What the return rests on

    Return on equity split three ways, before tax so that differing tax rates do not muddy a comparison between rivals. Two companies can post near-identical returns by opposite routes — one on a thin margin and a fast asset cycle, the other on a fat margin and a slow one. They are completely different businesses, and the headline number hides it.

    Growth: the industry's, or its own?

    Reported growth cannot distinguish a firm riding a rising industry from one taking share in a falling one. Splitting it does. Growth is also not evidence of an attractive industry — some of the fastest-growing have been among the least profitable.

    Five forces — what the filings can and cannot tell you

    The forces are tied to the income statements and balance sheets of an industry's participants: structure defines the gap between revenues and costs. Financial data can evidence some of that gap. It cannot observe buyer concentration, switching costs or entry barriers directly, so those are marked as needing outside evidence rather than quietly guessed at.

    How hard is this to copy?

    A rival's chance of matching any single activity is usually below one, and the probabilities compound across an interlocked system. Positions built on systems of activities are far more durable than those built on any one of them.

    Executive summary

    A filing never states a strategy in so many words, but it records every capital decision behind one, and it reports the result. What follows is read off the filings and off the company's peer set — nothing here is an estimate.

    Where the money actually goes

    Stated priorities are not observable in a filing; funded ones are. Each bar is a year's operating cash flow and how it was spent. The total can pass 100% — a company is allowed to outspend what it earns — so the axis is not clamped and the shortfall is named.

    Objectives against the peer set

    There are no published corporate targets in a filing, so these are not the company's own objectives. Each meter is a financial result placed against the same industry peers used throughout, which is the measurable half of the question.

    Risk

    Financial risk is measurable from the statements and is measured here. Strategic, operational and regulatory risk are not — a severity-and-likelihood grid drawn from financial data alone would be a chart of an opinion, so what cannot be evidenced is listed rather than plotted.
